Automated Sorting & Labeling System – Time Study and Automation ROI

Time-studied 48.0 labor-hr/day of automatable work and showed a $3.0M sorting system doesn't pay back at current throughput.

Answered one question for plant leadership: how much warehouse labor goes into each pound and case today, and how much could a conveyor-based sorting and labeling system realistically remove?

Time Study Labor-Cost Modeling ROI / NPV Excel
Problem Definition & Scope

Scoped to the same-day JIT/STD shipping workflow – the most labor-intensive and the best conveyor fit. The reasoning: a system able to handle that workflow would absorb the remaining make-to-stock volume with room to spare.

Method
  • Measured, not assumed: floor time studies gave labor-minutes per case for four workflow types. Applied to the daily case mix (2,551 cases/day at a 21 lb weighted-average case), 48.0 labor-hr/day – 30% of all warehouse labor – was automation-addressable.
  • Netted out what automation won't remove: a residual manual-workload model (monitoring, staging, sensor/scale checks, waste removal, maintenance) totaled 25.4 labor-hr/day, leaving 22.6 hr/day genuinely automatable.
  • Discounted savings to what's capturable: applied a reduced 15% overhead factor and modeled capture-rate scenarios by whole-FTE increments.
Results

Against a $3.0M installed cost, ROI ran 5.9% (16.8-yr payback) at current throughput and 9.96% (10.0-yr payback) at +25% throughput. The 15-year NPV stayed negative at an 8% hurdle rate in both cases, which supported a documented "not at this throughput" recommendation.

Equipment Capacity Analysis

Found that changeover frequency, not machine performance, was what held most lines below rated capacity.

Benchmarked actual throughput against rated design capacity across 11 production lines and 30+ machines.

Capacity Utilization Bottleneck Identification Excel
Problem Definition

How far below rated design capacity do the lines actually run, and what is holding them there?

Method

Triangulated three data sources rather than trusting any one: floor time studies, OEM design data books, and operator interviews.

Finding

Most lines ran materially below rated capacity. The gap traced back to frequent product changeovers (anywhere from a few hours to every other day). Changeover frequency, not machine performance, was the constraint worth attacking.

Layout Planning Production Continuity AutoCAD SolidWorks
Constraints

Approach

Coupled Electromagnetic-Thermal Modeling of Microwave Heating

Built a Maxwell + transient heat-transfer model and checked it against hand calculations and an energy/power balance.

Frequency-domain Maxwell solve coupled to a transient heat-transfer model of a 2.45 GHz cavity with a 250 mL water load, in COMSOL Multiphysics.

COMSOL Multiphysics Maxwell + Heat Transfer Model Validation
Governing Equations & Assumptions

Maxwell's equations are solved in the frequency domain. The two timescales (GHz field, seconds of heating) are bridged by using the cycle-averaged resistive loss as the heat source in the transient heat-transfer model.

Q = ½ Re(J · E*)  →  ρCp ∂T/∂t = ∇·(k∇T) + Q

Water's temperature-dependent permittivity is captured with a Debye relaxation model, which reproduces the self-stabilizing feedback: absorption falls as the load heats.

Validation

Validated before trusting it: hand-calculated the penetration depth and the expected temperature rise, then checked the solved model against those figures and against an energy/power balance.

Design of Experiments – Factorial Study of Cooling Performance

Ice quantity drove ~95% of cooling-rate variability (model R² = 0.97); turned the fitted models into operating recommendations.

Full 2³ factorial experiment (8 combinations × 2 replicates = 16 randomized runs) on how ice geometry, ice mass, and carbonation drive cooling rate and temperature retention. IEE 572.

Design of Experiments ANOVA Regression Modeling JMP
My Contribution

Designed the experiment, ran all 16 trials, and did the full statistical analysis and write-up.

Analysis

Half-normal plots, ANOVA, regression modeling, residual diagnostics, and response-surface plots in JMP.

Results
  • Cooling rate: ice quantity dominated (~95% of variability, p < .0001). Ice shape was smaller but real (~3%, p = .0212). Carbonation was not significant. Model R² = 0.97.
  • Retention time: ice quantity again dominant (~77%, p < .0001). Ice shape and the carbonation × shape interaction were each ~9% and significant. Model R² = 0.94.
  • Closed the loop: reduced the fitted models to operating recommendations rather than stopping at the statistics.

Thermal Simulation of Additive Manufacturing Processes

Showed that heat accumulation from a revisited track, not higher input power, pushed the melt pool ~475 K above liquidus.

Two FEA studies (Jan – Apr 2025): layer-by-layer heat buildup in FDM printing (ANSYS), and a moving laser source on a Ti-6Al-4V powder bed (COMSOL).

ANSYS COMSOL Transient Thermal FEA Additive Manufacturing
FDM (ANSYS)

Modeled layer-by-layer heat buildup with the element activation method and compared ABS to PA12. ABS retained more heat and had a larger heat-affected zone. PA12 cooled faster and had better thermal stability.

Laser Powder Bed (COMSOL)

Moving Gaussian heat source over a 0.4 mm Ti-6Al-4V powder layer at 30 W absorbed power and 800 mm/s, with conduction, convection, and radiation boundary conditions and localized 40 µm mesh refinement.

Scan-Strategy Result

A linear pass peaked at ~2264 K. A full circular lap reached ~2405 K, about 475 K above liquidus. The difference came from heat accumulating along a revisited track, not from higher input power.
